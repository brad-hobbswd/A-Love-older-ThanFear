CHAPTER 14

The Politics of Biblical Interpretation

Fear often disguises itself as certainty long before communities realize interpretation has already become shaped by power, control, and anxiety.

1 Samuel 20:17

“And Jonathan caused David to swear again, because he loved him: for he loved him as he loved his own soul.”

Few people realize how deeply politics influence theology until they begin studying which interpretations religious communities defend most fiercely and why.

Politics does not always mean governments, elections, or public policy. Politics also lives inside institutions, traditions, denominations, pulpits, classrooms, and social expectations. Politics shapes which questions feel safe to ask, which interpretations become acceptable, which voices gain authority, and which conversations communities quietly discourage before they fully begin.

The story of David and Jonathan exists inside that tension.

Across centuries, their relationship became more than a biblical narrative alone. The story slowly transformed into a theological pressure point where larger anxieties surrounding masculinity, sexuality, authority, holiness, and cultural control all began colliding at once.

Fear rarely stays isolated. Fear spreads outward into systems.

Many approached the story already carrying fears about what certain interpretations might mean for doctrine, church structure, social expectations, or moral boundaries.

That pressure shaped interpretation profoundly.

In many religious traditions, preserving specific theological systems became closely tied to preserving social order itself. Certain understandings of gender, marriage, masculinity, and family structure were treated not merely as interpretations, but as foundations protecting entire communities from perceived moral collapse.

Once that happened, passages involving emotional intimacy between men often became dangerous territory because readers feared what acknowledging tenderness too openly might disrupt.

Some interpreters therefore approached David and Jonathan already determined to keep the relationship safely contained before the emotional complexity of the text could create uncomfortable questions.

Friendship became acceptable as long as tenderness remained limited.

Covenant became acceptable as long as emotional intimacy never sounded too deep.

Grief became acceptable as long as readers never lingered too long inside David’s lament.

The conversation was often controlled before it even began.

This does not mean every traditional interpretation emerged from malicious intent. Many believers sincerely desired to protect what they believed Scripture taught about holiness, morality, and faithfulness. Yet sincerity and fear can exist together more easily than people sometimes realize.

Entire religious cultures can become shaped by fear while believing they are defending truth.

Throughout history, marginalized groups often experienced this reality firsthand. Questions surrounding race, women in ministry, slavery, mental health, and countless other issues frequently revealed how strongly cultural power influenced biblical interpretation.

Communities sometimes defended interpretations passionately for generations before later recognizing how deeply fear, politics, or social control shaped those readings.

The church is still vulnerable to that pattern now.

Many LGBTQIA+ believers know intimately what happens when theology becomes entangled with fear driven politics. Some grew up inside churches where identity itself became treated as a threat before anyone paused long enough to hear their stories honestly.

Others learned quickly which questions could cost them acceptance, ministry opportunities, friendships, or belonging.

The emotional weight surrounding David and Jonathan often became part of that silence.

Some readers recognized tenderness within the text immediately yet learned not to speak about it aloud. Others sensed emotional complexity but watched church communities react so defensively that silence felt safer than honesty.

Fear taught people to distrust their own reading of the text.

Yet Scripture itself remained unchanged beneath all the tension.

David still loved Jonathan as his own soul.

Jonathan still stripped himself of the robe and bow.

They still kissed and wept together.

David still declared Jonathan’s love wonderful and surpassing.

The text survived every political struggle surrounding it.

That survival matters because truth often becomes clearest precisely where communities work hardest to control interpretation.

Politics often asks how interpretation will preserve power.

Love asks whether interpretation remains honest.

This book does not argue that every reader must reach identical conclusions about David and Jonathan. Honest believers continue disagreeing sincerely. The deeper concern involves whether fear, institutional pressure, or political anxiety prevented certain conversations from ever unfolding openly in the first place.

Scripture deserves better than fear driven interpretation.

Wounded people deserve better than silence driven theology.

Perhaps one of the most sacred acts readers can offer Scripture is the willingness to approach the text without already demanding that the conclusions protect every system built around it.

Honest study requires humility strong enough to admit that history, politics, and fear have influenced interpretation before and may still be influencing interpretation now.

David and Jonathan remain within Scripture waiting beyond all of those pressures.

Their story survived kingdoms.

Their story survived institutions.

Their story survived politics.

And perhaps sacred truth has always survived most clearly wherever love proved stronger than fear.
