CHAPTER 17

Spiritual Trauma and Sacred Silence

Some wounds are formed not by losing faith, but by surviving environments where honesty never felt safe beside belonging.

1 John 4:18

“There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear.”

Not every spiritual wound comes from losing faith.

Some wounds come from trying desperately to keep faith while surviving environments where honesty felt unsafe.

Many LGBTQIA+ believers know that tension intimately. Some grew up loving God deeply while simultaneously learning to fear what would happen if certain questions, emotions, or truths about themselves ever became visible inside church spaces. The fear was not always spoken directly. Sometimes the silence carried the message clearly enough on its own.

People learned what could be said.

People learned what must remain hidden.

People learned how quickly tenderness could become dangerous once connected to certain identities.

Over time, many believers developed two separate lives. One life existed publicly inside church where survival depended upon careful restraint. The other life existed privately beneath prayer, fear, loneliness, exhaustion, and the constant pressure of hiding pieces of the heart believed unacceptable before God.

That kind of division wounds people deeply.

Spiritual trauma does not always arrive through dramatic moments alone. Sometimes trauma forms slowly through years of hearing love preached while never feeling fully safe inside the places preaching it. Sometimes trauma grows through constant vigilance, through monitoring every friendship, every gesture, every emotion, every conversation, and every prayer in order to avoid rejection. Sometimes trauma forms when believers learn that belonging feels conditional upon silence.

Fear creates exhausting forms of survival.

Many queer believers became experts at shrinking themselves spiritually. Some stopped speaking honestly in Bible studies. Some avoided emotional closeness entirely. Some buried tenderness beneath shame until they no longer recognized their own hearts clearly anymore. Others remained outwardly faithful while inwardly carrying grief no one around them could see.

“Perhaps one of the holiest things the church can do is create spaces where people no longer have to choose between honesty and belonging.”

The church often misunderstood this silence.

Silence was sometimes mistaken for holiness.

Silence was sometimes mistaken for agreement.

Silence was sometimes mistaken for peace.

Yet many people remained silent simply because survival required it.

This chapter is not written to accuse every church or every believer of intentional harm. Many pastors loved sincerely according to what they believed was faithful teaching. Many communities genuinely desired holiness, truth, and obedience to God. Yet sincere intentions do not erase the emotional consequences fear can produce inside vulnerable people.

Fear driven theology often leaves believers trapped between longing for God and fearing rejection from the people claiming to represent Him.

That tension becomes spiritually devastating over time.

Some LGBTQIA+ believers eventually walked away from church not because they hated God, but because they could no longer survive environments where honesty and belonging felt permanently separated from one another. Others stayed while carrying invisible wounds for decades. Some lost relationships with family. Some lost ministry opportunities. Some lost trust in themselves entirely because fear taught them to question every natural affection, every emotional connection, and every longing for intimacy or safety.

Many readers encounter David and Jonathan while carrying those wounds quietly.

Perhaps that is why the story feels so emotionally significant to many people. Scripture preserves tenderness between men without shame attached to the language itself. Covenant remains visible. Tears remain visible. Love remains visible. For wounded readers accustomed to emotional suppression inside religious environments, that honesty can feel both comforting and painful at the same time.

Comforting because the tenderness survived.

Painful because many communities taught believers to fear tenderness so deeply.

Sacred silence becomes dangerous when silence protects fear more than truth.

Throughout the Gospels, Jesus repeatedly confronted religious systems that burdened people spiritually while failing to offer mercy alongside truth. He moved toward wounded people compassionately even when others believed distance protected holiness better. Again and again, Christ exposed the difference between religious control and genuine spiritual healing.

Healing requires honesty.

Healing requires spaces where wounded people no longer fear immediate rejection for speaking openly about their questions, grief, confusion, or longing. Healing requires communities willing to listen before rushing toward condemnation or defensiveness. Healing requires believers to recognize that theology affects real human lives, not abstract debates alone.

Many queer believers still carry sacred silence inside them even now.

Some still pray carefully worded prayers because fear taught them honesty was dangerous before God.

Some still attend church while feeling emotionally invisible.

Some still wonder whether tenderness itself will always be treated as suspicious within faith communities.

Those wounds matter.

This chapter exists because spiritual trauma deserves acknowledgment rather than avoidance. Wounded believers deserve more than dismissal. Silence may protect institutions temporarily, but silence rarely heals human hearts.

Perhaps one of the holiest things the church can do is create spaces where people no longer have to choose between honesty and belonging.

And perhaps sacred love was never meant to produce fear heavy enough to silence people searching sincerely for God in the first place.

Sacred love was never meant to produce fear heavy enough to silence people searching sincerely for God.
