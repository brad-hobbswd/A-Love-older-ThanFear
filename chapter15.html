CHAPTER 15

When Readers Bring Fear to Scripture

Sometimes the greatest barrier between readers and Scripture is not the text itself, but the fear already carried into the reading before the first verse is ever opened.

1 John 4:18

“There is no fear in love; but perfect love casteth out fear.”

Every reader approaches Scripture carrying something into the text long before interpretation begins.

Tradition enters the reading. Family teaching enters the reading. Church culture enters the reading. Personal wounds, fears, assumptions, anxieties, loyalties, and experiences all arrive quietly beside the reader before the first verse is ever examined carefully.

No one reads completely untouched by prior influence.

That reality does not make honest interpretation impossible, yet recognizing those influences matters deeply because fear often shapes interpretation most powerfully when readers fail to notice fear has already entered the room.

The story of David and Jonathan reveals this tension clearly.

Many believers encounter their relationship already trained to become uncomfortable whenever emotional tenderness between men appears within Scripture. Some readers were raised inside theological environments where masculinity became associated almost entirely with emotional restraint, authority, distance, and suspicion toward vulnerability. Others inherited church cultures where LGBTQIA+ people were discussed primarily through fear, condemnation, or silence long before passages involving covenantal intimacy were ever examined honestly.

Under those conditions, readers often approach David and Jonathan defensively before the text itself has fully spoken.

Fear rarely announces itself openly. More often, fear disguises itself as certainty.

Readers begin softening emotional language automatically. Covenant becomes reduced to political alliance alone. Grief becomes explained away quickly. Tears become uncomfortable. Physical tenderness becomes suspicious. Love becomes acceptable only after strict emotional limits are imposed upon the text immediately.

Yet Scripture itself frequently refuses that emotional distance.

The biblical narrative slows repeatedly around David and Jonathan with extraordinary tenderness. Souls are described as knit together. Covenants are formed through love. Tears are preserved openly. Kisses are recorded without apology. David’s lament speaks with emotional intensity generations still struggle to explain comfortably.

The text itself remains remarkably unafraid of emotional vulnerability.

That contrast raises an important question: if Scripture preserves these moments openly, why do so many readers instinctively feel pressure to contain them?

Part of the answer lies within fear shaped by culture rather than by the biblical text itself.

Throughout history, communities often developed emotional boundaries defining which forms of tenderness felt acceptable between men and which forms immediately triggered anxiety or suspicion. Those cultural boundaries gradually influenced theology, preaching, translation, and interpretation until many believers no longer recognized where Scripture ended and inherited fear began.

Fear changes vision.

Readers shaped by fear often begin seeing danger where tenderness exists naturally. Questions become threatening. Emotional honesty becomes controversial. Curiosity becomes treated as compromise. Theological certainty becomes valued more highly than careful listening.

Yet honest biblical study requires humility strong enough to recognize that readers themselves are not emotionally neutral interpreters standing outside history untouched by cultural influence.

Every generation inherits assumptions.

Some assumptions clarify Scripture faithfully. Others distort the text quietly beneath the appearance of certainty. Wisdom requires learning the difference.

This does not mean every interpretation carries equal weight or that all conclusions become automatically correct simply because fear exists somewhere within human culture. Honest study still requires discernment, historical awareness, theological seriousness, linguistic care, and humility before sacred text.

What this chapter asks instead is something simpler and far more personal:

What fears might readers already be carrying into the text before interpretation even begins?

That question matters because fear rarely remains passive once introduced into theology. Fear teaches readers where not to look. Fear teaches communities which questions feel forbidden. Fear teaches believers which emotions appear dangerous. Fear teaches wounded people to hide parts of themselves before Scripture ever has the opportunity to speak honestly into their lives.

Many LGBTQIA+ believers know this reality intimately.

Some spent years approaching Scripture already terrified of what they might discover about themselves, terrified of rejection, terrified of losing family, community, ministry, belonging, or spiritual safety if honesty ever became visible. Under that kind of pressure, fear does not simply shape interpretation academically. Fear shapes survival.

That pain deserves compassion rather than dismissal.

The tragedy is not merely that difficult conversations exist surrounding David and Jonathan. The deeper tragedy is how often fear prevented honest conversations from happening at all.

Perhaps that is why this story continues speaking so powerfully across generations. Beneath theology and debate stands something profoundly human: the longing to know whether love, tenderness, faithfulness, covenant, and truth can exist without fear demanding silence first.

The story of David and Jonathan does not force every reader toward identical conclusions.

What the story does require, however, is honesty about the emotional intensity Scripture itself chose to preserve and honesty about the fears readers sometimes bring into the text before interpretation begins.

Because sometimes the clearest obstacle between people and Scripture is not what the Bible says, but what fear has already convinced them they are allowed to see.

Fear can shape interpretation so quietly that generations mistake inherited anxiety for the voice of Scripture itself.
