CHAPTER 8

Kisses, Tears, and Ancient Masculinity

Few things reveal the distance between the ancient world and modern culture more clearly than the way men expressed affection, grief, loyalty, and emotional devotion publicly.

“The tears remained. The kisses remained. The grief remained.”

CHAPTER EIGHT

Ancient tenderness often survived inside Scripture long after cultures learned to fear vulnerability.

Few things reveal the distance between the ancient world and modern culture more clearly than the way men expressed affection, grief, loyalty, and emotional devotion publicly. Many modern readers approach Scripture carrying assumptions shaped by generations of emotional restraint where tenderness between men often becomes uncomfortable once it grows too visible.

As a result, passages involving tears, embraces, kisses, and vulnerable expressions of love are frequently rushed past or explained away before readers pause long enough to consider how ordinary such actions often were within the biblical world.

The relationship between David and Jonathan stands directly inside that tension.

Throughout their story, Scripture repeatedly preserves moments of emotional openness modern audiences are not always prepared to encounter honestly. Their covenant is not hidden behind cold political language. Their devotion is not emotionally distant. The narrative allows readers to witness grief, affection, protection, weeping, longing, and visible tenderness unfolding between two men standing within a kingdom increasingly consumed by fear.

One of the clearest examples appears in 1 Samuel 20:41:

“And they kissed one another, and wept one with another, until David exceeded.”

The moment unfolds after Jonathan secretly warns David that Saul fully intends to kill him. Danger now surrounds every part of David’s future. The two men meet in the field knowing separation may soon become permanent.

Scripture does not present the scene with emotional restraint. Instead, the text slows down and allows readers to remain inside the grief of the moment fully.

They kiss.

They weep together.

David breaks down openly.

Ancient readers would not necessarily have interpreted this scene through the same lens modern Western culture often does today. Kissing between men existed throughout the ancient Near East as a sign of affection, covenant loyalty, familial devotion, greeting, farewell, mourning, or deep emotional attachment.

Public grief between men also carried no automatic stigma within biblical literature. Men cried openly throughout Scripture. Warriors lamented one another publicly. Kings mourned visibly. Emotional expression was not treated as weakness incompatible with masculinity.

David himself embodies this repeatedly throughout his life. He dances before God openly. He writes poetry overflowing with anguish, joy, repentance, longing, and grief. He mourns publicly for Saul and Jonathan. He cries over Absalom.

Scripture never portrays his emotional vulnerability as diminishing his strength.

Modern culture often separates tenderness from masculinity.

Ancient Scripture rarely does.

That distinction matters enormously when reading David and Jonathan because many modern readers instinctively react to emotional intimacy between men with suspicion before allowing the text to exist naturally within its own historical context.

Some immediately attempt to reduce every display of affection into purely formal custom while others interpret every moment exclusively through modern romantic categories. Yet the ancient world often allowed emotional, covenantal, and relational complexity to exist more openly than contemporary culture tends to permit.

The biblical text itself refuses emotional distance.

The tears remain in the story.

The kisses remain in the story.

The grief remains in the story.

Fear often enters later through interpretation.

Many cultures shaped by rigid expectations surrounding masculinity became increasingly uncomfortable with male vulnerability over time. Emotional restraint became associated with strength. Tenderness became guarded carefully. Affection between men became heavily restricted or treated with suspicion.

Readers shaped by those cultural expectations often approach biblical passages defensively because the emotional openness within Scripture no longer feels familiar to them.

Yet perhaps the discomfort says more about modern fear than about ancient relationships.

The relationship between David and Jonathan unfolds within a world where covenant loyalty carried deep emotional significance and where grief was expressed openly rather than hidden behind emotional silence.

Their story does not become less meaningful because ancient masculinity functioned differently than modern masculinity. Rather, understanding that world allows readers to encounter the text more honestly without forcing contemporary anxieties back onto ancient Scripture.

This chapter does not attempt to erase the interpretive tensions surrounding David and Jonathan. Readers continue disagreeing about how their relationship should ultimately be understood.

Some view these moments entirely within the framework of covenant friendship.

Others believe the emotional intensity points toward romantic love.

Some recognize elements impossible to separate neatly into modern categories at all.

What remains undeniable is the tenderness Scripture preserves between them.

The Bible could have told this story without tears.

The Bible could have omitted the kisses.

The Bible could have restrained the grief.

Instead, Scripture preserved every detail.

Perhaps that preservation matters because love powerful enough to survive fear always leaves traces history cannot fully erase.
