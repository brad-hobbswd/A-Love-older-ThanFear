PART III

Fear and Interpretation

The language survived. The covenant survived. The grief survived. Yet generations of readers still struggled with the emotional honesty Scripture preserved openly. This section confronts the fears that entered interpretation long after the text itself was written.

“Fear rarely erases Scripture. More often, fear teaches readers how to look away.”

PART INTRODUCTION

Sometimes fear enters interpretation before readers realize it arrived.

By the time readers reach this point in the journey, one reality becomes difficult to ignore: the emotional depth surrounding David and Jonathan does not come from modern imagination imposed onto Scripture later. The language is already there. The covenant is already there. The grief is already there. The tenderness survived centuries because the biblical text itself preserved it openly.

So why has their relationship made so many readers uncomfortable?

That question leads directly into this next section.

Interpretation is never shaped by Scripture alone. Human fear often enters the conversation long before readers realize it has arrived. Fear influences theology. Fear influences tradition. Fear influences translation, preaching, doctrine, masculinity, sexuality, and the emotional boundaries communities establish around acceptable forms of love and vulnerability.

Many believers inherited interpretations already shaped by generations of discomfort surrounding tenderness between men. Some church traditions responded to David and Jonathan cautiously, carefully limiting emotional possibilities before difficult questions could fully emerge. Others approached the relationship defensively, treating honest curiosity itself as dangerous. Over time, fear often became woven into interpretation so deeply that many readers no longer recognized the difference between the biblical text and the anxieties later generations carried into it.

This section explores that tension honestly.

Together we will examine how church history, theology, masculinity, cultural fear, and religious discomfort shaped the way David and Jonathan were understood across centuries. We will also confront the painful reality that fear based theology has deeply wounded countless LGBTQIA+ believers who spent years searching Scripture while wondering whether love, tenderness, belonging, and faithfulness could ever exist without shame attached to them.

This part of the journey may feel uncomfortable at times because fear rarely enjoys being exposed. Yet honest study requires more than examining ancient words alone. Honest study also asks what later generations feared enough to soften, restrict, or explain away once those words survived into the present.

The goal is not accusation.

The goal is understanding.

Because sometimes the clearest way to see what Scripture truly says is by noticing what history became afraid to hear.
