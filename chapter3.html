CHAPTER 3

Love, Covenant, and the Ancient World

Modern readers often approach David and Jonathan through assumptions shaped by contemporary culture, yet the ancient world understood covenant, loyalty, tenderness, and relational devotion very differently.

1 Samuel 18:3

“Then Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul.”

The ancient world did not think exactly like the modern world.

That reality sounds obvious at first, yet readers often forget how deeply modern assumptions shape the way Scripture is interpreted. Contemporary culture carries particular expectations surrounding masculinity, emotion, friendship, sexuality, affection, loyalty, and identity. Those assumptions quietly influence interpretation long before many readers realize they have entered the text already carrying cultural conclusions with them.

The relationship between David and Jonathan becomes especially difficult for modern readers because the ancient biblical world frequently expressed emotional devotion between men with far greater openness than many modern cultures later became comfortable allowing.

Men embraced openly within Scripture.

Men wept publicly.

Men kissed in greeting and farewell.

Warriors mourned one another poetically with intense emotional language.

The biblical narrative does not treat tenderness automatically as weakness or suspicion.

Ancient readers often feared vulnerability less than modern readers do.

That distinction matters deeply when approaching David and Jonathan honestly because many contemporary readers instinctively try to impose emotional restrictions onto the relationship before the text itself ever does.

Part of that tension emerges from the meaning of covenant within the ancient world.

Modern readers often hear the word covenant and imagine little more than agreement or promise. Ancient societies understood covenant far more seriously. Covenants established identity, obligation, protection, kinship, inheritance expectations, loyalty, and sacred relational bonds carrying spiritual and communal weight.

Covenants shaped lives.

Throughout the Old Testament, covenant language appears repeatedly in relationships carrying profound seriousness. God establishes covenant with Israel. Families form covenant obligations. Political alliances depend upon covenant faithfulness. Kings govern through covenant responsibility. The language carried relational depth modern readers frequently underestimate.

Jonathan and David’s covenant therefore exists within a sacred framework rather than casual emotional attachment alone.

Yet covenant and emotional devotion are never separated cleanly within their story. Scripture repeatedly joins love and covenant together when describing them. Jonathan loves David “as his own soul.” Their covenant forms because of that love. Their loyalty survives political danger because covenant continues binding them together even when fear surrounds them from every side.

The ancient world would have recognized the extraordinary nature of such devotion between potential rivals.

Jonathan is Saul’s son.

Jonathan is heir to the throne.

Jonathan stands directly between David and kingship.

Ancient succession crises frequently produced violence, betrayal, assassination, and bloodshed. Kingdoms were protected through dynasties. Sons defended inheritance fiercely because survival often depended upon it. Political reality alone suggests Jonathan should resent David’s rise immediately.

Instead, Jonathan repeatedly protects David.

That reversal matters enormously within the narrative itself.

Readers sometimes forget how radical Jonathan’s covenant loyalty actually becomes in the context of ancient kingship. Jonathan willingly places covenant above personal ambition and dynastic expectation. He recognizes David’s future while surrendering his own earthly claim to power without apparent bitterness.

That kind of devotion would have appeared astonishing within the political realities of the ancient Near East.

The covenant also involves symbolic action carrying enormous meaning.

1 Samuel 18:4 records Jonathan giving David his robe, garments, sword, bow, and girdle. Ancient readers would immediately recognize these items as symbols connected to identity, status, inheritance, warfare, and princely authority. Jonathan’s actions are not emotionally empty gestures. The exchange carries covenantal and political significance woven together.

Some scholars view this moment primarily as dynastic recognition. Others see emotional intimacy inseparable from covenant devotion itself. Still others recognize that ancient relationships sometimes carried layers modern categories struggle to separate neatly.

The text itself leaves room for tension.

Modern readers often demand immediate clarity because ambiguity creates discomfort. Ancient literature, however, frequently preserved emotional complexity without reducing relationships into rigid categories designed to satisfy later audiences.

That complexity becomes even more important when considering masculinity within the biblical world.

Many modern Western cultures gradually developed forms of masculinity emphasizing emotional restraint, distance, stoicism, and suspicion toward tenderness between men. Those assumptions became so normalized that many believers eventually projected them backward onto Scripture itself.

Yet biblical masculinity often looked very different.

David composes grief poetry publicly.

Joseph weeps openly.

Jacob kisses and embraces.

The prophets lament emotionally.

Warriors mourn aloud.

The biblical world frequently treats emotional expression as profoundly human rather than automatically threatening.

Recognizing that historical difference does not answer every interpretive question surrounding David and Jonathan, yet it does remove one major obstacle preventing many readers from hearing the story honestly. Ancient tenderness existed within a world less anxious about emotional vulnerability than many modern cultures later became.

That realization should caution readers against rushing too quickly toward defensive conclusions designed primarily to protect modern discomfort rather than understand the ancient text carefully on its own terms.

The relationship between David and Jonathan emerges from a world shaped by covenant, loyalty, kinship, warfare, succession anxiety, sacred promises, and emotional openness unfamiliar to many contemporary readers.

The challenge is not forcing the story artificially into modern political arguments.

The challenge is learning to encounter the ancient world honestly enough to allow Scripture to speak from within its own cultural and emotional framework first.

Because the deeper readers move into that ancient world, the more difficult the relationship between David and Jonathan becomes to reduce into something emotionally shallow, politically ordinary, or spiritually insignificant.

Their covenant carried weight.

Their loyalty carried cost.

Their tenderness carried visibility.

And Scripture chose to preserve all of it openly.

Ancient covenant relationships often carried emotional depth modern readers were taught to overlook too quickly.
